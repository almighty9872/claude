Why We're Catholic

Whether you believe or not, this page isn't trying to convince you in a single sentence. It tries to show, step by step, that the Catholic faith speaks to reason as much as to the heart: starting from the most basic questions (what is true? where did the universe come from?) and going all the way to Jesus and the Church he founded. At each step we also take on the strongest objection a skeptic might raise.

Step 1 of 5

Truth and God

This step isn't about religion yet, but about reality: that the universe exists at all, that it is so finely tuned, and that we can tell good from evil make a Creator the most reasonable explanation.

Objective Truth

Isn't truth different for everyone?

Tastes differ from person to person, but facts don't; and no one actually lives as a moral relativist.

  • The sentence “there is no absolute truth” presents itself as an absolute truth, so it refutes itself.
  • No one is content with a bridge being sound “for me”; we want medicine, planes and bridges to be really safe.
  • No one seriously argues that torturing children could be “right for some cultures”; we know some things are wrong everywhere and always.
  • So the question of God isn't a matter of taste either, but a real question with a true or false answer, and it deserves an honest search.
A skeptic might askBut different societies have held different moral codes throughout history.

Answer: True, but those differences usually come from disagreements about facts, not about basic values: almost every culture praises courage, loyalty and justice and condemns betrayal and cowardice. And disagreement doesn't mean no view is correct; some people once thought the earth was flat, but it was round all the same. When slavery was abolished, we don't say societies merely “changed” but that they “improved,” which assumes a real good we are measuring against.

The Beginning of the Universe

Couldn't the universe just exist on its own?

Modern science says the universe had a beginning, and everything that begins to exist has a cause.

  • The first person to propose that the universe is expanding (in 1927), and that it began from a single moment, a “primeval atom” (in 1931), was a Belgian Catholic priest and physicist: Georges Lemaître. Today the idea is known as the Big Bang model.
  • If time, space and matter themselves came into being at that beginning, their cause must lie outside time, space and matter: timeless, immaterial and immensely powerful.
  • “Nothing” can't produce anything; even a quantum vacuum is a “something” with laws and energy, not nothing in the true sense.
  • That description of a first cause matches, strikingly, what religions call God.
A skeptic might askThen who created God?

Answer: The argument doesn't say “everything that exists has a cause,” but “everything that begins to exist has a cause.” The universe began; God, by definition, did not: he is outside time. An endless chain of causes explains nothing; at some point we have to reach a being that needs nothing else in order to exist. The real question isn't whether such a being exists, but whether it is a blind force or a person.

Fine-Tuning

Couldn't life be just a lucky accident?

The basic constants of the universe appear to be set with astonishing precision to allow life.

  • If the strong nuclear force were different by just a few percent, stars couldn't burn, or the carbon and oxygen life needs couldn't form.
  • The cosmological constant, which governs how fast the universe expands, sits in an extraordinarily narrow range by physicists' reckoning; a little larger and no galaxy could ever have formed.
  • The atheist astronomer Fred Hoyle looked at this picture and wrote: “A common sense interpretation of the facts suggests that a superintellect has monkeyed with physics.”
  • Design is the most natural explanation of this precision; when we see someone win the lottery thousands of times in a row, we don't say “luck” either.
A skeptic might askMaybe there are countless universes and we just happen to be in a lucky one.

Answer: The multiverse isn't an observed fact but an untestable hypothesis; assuming infinitely many unseen universes to explain fine-tuning is a far bigger leap of faith than assuming one Creator. And a universe-generating “machine” would itself need finely tuned laws: the problem isn't solved, only pushed back a step.

The Problem of Evil

If a good God exists, why is there so much suffering?

This is the most honest and the heaviest question; Christianity answers it not with a formula but with the cross.

  • God gave us real freedom, because love that is forced isn't love; most of the evil people do to one another is that freedom misused.
  • Without God, even the word “evil” loses its meaning: if the universe is only blind processes, there is no standard by which an atrocity is really “wrong.” Our outrage at evil assumes that a real good exists.
  • From our limited view we can't be sure that a given suffering has no purpose; but we know that God can bring good even out of the greatest evil.
  • Most importantly, the Christian God doesn't watch suffering from afar: in Christ he went through pain, betrayal, torture and death himself, and defeated death through the resurrection.
A skeptic might askAn all-powerful God would simply remove all suffering right away.

Answer: The only way to do that would be to remove free will, and with it love, courage and forgiveness. Christian hope isn't that suffering lasts forever but that it will end: “He will wipe every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain” (Revelation 21:4). God will defeat evil not instantly, but for certain.

Step 2 of 5

Jesus and the Bible

If a Creator exists, might he have made himself known? Christianity answers with an event that can be tested by history: the death and resurrection of Jesus.

The Historical Jesus

Did Jesus really live, or is he a legend?

That Jesus lived and was crucified under Pontius Pilate is accepted by nearly all historians, believers and unbelievers alike.

  • The Roman historian Tacitus (around AD 116) writes that Christians take their name from Christus, who was executed under the governor Pontius Pilate during the reign of Tiberius.
  • The Jewish historian Josephus mentions “James, the brother of Jesus who was called Christ,” and Pliny the Younger tells the emperor about Christians who sang hymns to Christ “as to a god.”
  • Even the agnostic New Testament scholar Bart Ehrman considers Jesus' existence beyond serious historical dispute.
  • More than 5,800 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament have come down to us; no other ancient work is preserved in so many, or such early, copies.
A skeptic might askThe Gospels were written long after Jesus, and by believers.

Answer: The Gospels were written a few decades after Jesus, while eyewitnesses were still alive; by the standards of ancient history that is very short (the earliest surviving full accounts of Alexander the Great were written about three hundred years after his death). And the Gospels are full of embarrassing details no invented story would include: the apostles' cowardice, Peter's denial, and the fact that the first witnesses of the empty tomb were women, whose testimony carried little weight at the time.

Who Jesus Claimed to Be

Couldn't Jesus just have been a good teacher?

What Jesus said about himself rules that option out.

  • He claimed to forgive sins; those present were outraged because they knew only God has that authority (Mark 2:5-7).
  • He said “I and the Father are one” (John 10:30), called himself by the name God revealed to Moses, “I AM” (John 8:58), and his hearers tried to stone him for it.
  • Someone who makes such claims is either a deluded madman, a deliberate liar, or exactly who he says he is. The depth of Jesus' teaching and the integrity of his life don't fit the first two.
  • As C. S. Lewis put it: “Let us not come with any patronising nonsense about His being a great human teacher. He has not left that open to us. He did not intend to.”
A skeptic might askMaybe later generations put these words in his mouth.

Answer: Belief in Jesus' divinity goes back very early: Paul's letters, written less than thirty years after Jesus' death, already speak of him as the Lord worshipped as God (Philippians 2:6-11). Legends take generations to form; here, while eyewitnesses were still alive, and among the Jews, the people most fiercely devoted to one God, a man began to be worshipped as God, which has no parallel.

The Resurrection

How can anyone believe a man rose from the dead?

Christianity is the one great religion that rests on a testable historical claim; as Paul said, “If Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile” (1 Corinthians 15:17).

  • Facts that even most skeptical historians accept: Jesus died on the cross and was buried; his tomb was soon found empty; his followers sincerely believed they had seen him risen.
  • The creed in 1 Corinthians 15:3-8 goes back, according to many scholars, to only a few years after the event and mentions more than five hundred witnesses; Paul reminds his readers that most of them were still alive and could be asked.
  • Two skeptics were transformed as well: Jesus' brother James, who didn't believe in him during his life, became leader of the church in Jerusalem; Paul, who persecuted Christians, became its greatest missionary.
  • A handful of frightened followers in hiding began, within weeks, to proclaim the resurrection openly in the very city where they could be killed for it; tradition holds that most of them died as martyrs for this testimony.
A skeptic might askMaybe the apostles hallucinated, or stole the body.

Answer: Hallucinations are private; five hundred people don't share the same one at the same time, and a hallucination doesn't empty a tomb. Had they stolen the body, they would all have gone to torture and death for a lie; people die for what they believe is true, but not for what they know is a lie. Their enemies could have ended everything by producing the body, and never did. The explanations that deny the resurrection can't account for all the facts at once.

Step 3 of 5

The Church and Sacraments

If Jesus rose, what he said matters; and he left behind not a book but a community: one Church built on the apostles, continuing unbroken to this day.

One Church and Peter

Isn't believing in Jesus enough? Why a Church?

Jesus entrusted his teaching not just to individuals but to a visible community with authority.

  • He told Peter, “You are Peter (rock), and on this rock I will build my church,” and gave him the keys of the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 16:18-19).
  • This Church had order from the first day: the apostles, the bishops they appointed to succeed them, and Peter's special role among them. Clement, bishop of Rome, was already writing with authority to the distant church of Corinth at the end of the first century.
  • Without a shared authority, every reader interprets the Bible differently; the thousands of separated Christian communities today are the result. Christ, though, prayed that his disciples “may all be one” (John 17:21).
  • Today's pope is the latest link in a chain of more than 260 that began with Peter and has continued unbroken for two thousand years; empires, kingdoms and ideologies have come and gone, and the chain has not broken.
A skeptic might askThe Church turned Jesus' simple message into an institution later on.

Answer: Quite the reverse: the institution was there before the New Testament. Jesus wrote no book; he chose twelve apostles and gave them authority to teach, to forgive and to celebrate the sacraments. The New Testament itself was written inside this Church and for its needs. Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, writing around AD 107, in the generation taught by the apostles' own disciples, was the first to use the phrase “Catholic Church,” and already described loyalty to the bishop as a matter of course.

Scripture and Tradition

Why isn't the Bible alone enough?

It was the Church that decided which books make up the Bible; the Book was born inside the Church.

  • The New Testament writings circulated separately for years; today's list of 27 books was settled at the end of the fourth century, at the councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397).
  • The Bible itself asks us to hold on to the teaching handed down by word of mouth as well as in writing: “Hold to the traditions which you were taught by us, either by word of mouth or by letter” (2 Thessalonians 2:15).
  • The Church sees Scripture, Sacred Tradition and the teaching authority that interprets them as one whole whose parts complete each other. All three come from the same source, Christ.
A skeptic might askThen the Church places itself above the Bible.

Answer: The Church sees itself not as the Bible's master but as its servant: it teaches, guards and explains it, but can't change it. Like a museum guarding a painting entrusted to it and telling it apart from forgeries: the museum didn't paint it, but it is the one able to recognize and protect it. Reading the Bible without the Church is like reading a family letter without ever knowing the family.

The Sacraments

Can water, bread and oil really have a spiritual effect?

God made us body and soul, so he gives his grace through signs we can see and touch.

  • Jesus himself touched people when he healed them and used mud and water; he commanded his disciples to baptize, to offer bread and wine “in remembrance of me,” and to forgive sins.
  • The seven sacraments accompany every important moment of life from birth to death: Baptism, Confirmation, the Eucharist, Confession, the Anointing of the Sick, Holy Orders and Marriage.
  • The first Christians didn't see the Eucharist as a symbol: around AD 107, Ignatius of Antioch was already criticizing those who denied that the Eucharist is “the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ.”
  • In Confession we hear with our own ears, through the priest, the words “your sins are forgiven”; this isn't just something we hope for inwardly but a concrete assurance Christ gives us.
A skeptic might askIsn't that a kind of magic?

Answer: Magic is a person trying to bend supernatural powers to their will by saying the right words. The sacraments are the opposite: no one is controlling anything; God is keeping his own promise. Their effect rests not on the priest's holiness or the grandeur of the ceremony but on Christ's word, and they don't force the heart of the one receiving them; they invite it.

Scandals in the Church

How can the Church be holy with so many scandals?

The Church's holiness comes not from the perfection of its members but from Christ; it is not a museum for saints but a hospital for sinners.

  • Sins such as abuse by clergy and its cover-up are real, grave and shameful; the Church must confess them and bring justice to victims. Popes have apologized for them again and again.
  • One of the twelve Jesus chose, Judas, betrayed him; Peter denied him three times. Christ knew from the start that the Church would be made up of flawed people.
  • A bad doctor doesn't discredit medicine; the truth of a teaching is measured by itself, not by those who fail to live it. Scandals show not that the Church's teaching is wrong but that it wasn't followed.
  • The same Church also raised up Teresa of Calcutta, Maximilian Kolbe and millions of holy people whose names we will never know.
A skeptic might askA two-thousand-year-old institution surviving is just good organization.

Answer: No human institution has survived twenty centuries, keeping the same teaching, despite so many failures by its own leaders, so many divisions and so much persecution. Jesus promised that “the gates of hell shall not prevail against it” (Matthew 16:18). That the Church has lived so long with such weak people is, if not a proof, at least a sign worth thinking about carefully.

Step 4 of 5

Saints and Sinners

A tree is known by its fruit: Christianity doesn't only claim to be true; it really transforms people and societies.

Known by Its Fruit

Has religion really done humanity any good?

Many institutions we take for granted today were born at the hands of the Catholic Church.

  • In the fourth century, Saint Basil founded a great complex in Caesarea (today's Kayseri) where the sick, the poor and travellers were cared for; many historians count it among the first hospitals in today's sense.
  • The first great universities of the Middle Ages (Paris, Oxford, Bologna) grew up under the Church's care. Gregor Mendel, the founder of modern genetics, was an Augustinian friar; Lemaître, who first proposed the Big Bang, was a priest.
  • The idea that every person, rich or poor, slave or free, is made in God's image and shares an equal dignity is one of the roots of human rights.
  • Today the Catholic Church still runs one of the largest networks of schools, hospitals and charities in the world.
A skeptic might askBut there were also the Crusades and the Inquisition.

Answer: Yes, and the Church has openly acknowledged the sins Christians committed in those periods; Saint John Paul II publicly asked forgiveness for them in 2000. But a teaching must be judged by itself, not by those who acted against it: the very standard by which we condemn those evils is the teaching of Christ, who commanded us to love even our enemies.

Grace and Good Works

Do you get to heaven by being a good person?

No: salvation isn't a reward we earn but God's free gift; but real faith shows itself in good works.

  • None of us can reach God's perfect goodness by our own effort; that is why God himself took the first step to reach us.
  • Many religions describe humanity's effort to reach God; Christianity is the story of God setting out to look for humanity, like the shepherd searching for his lost sheep (Luke 15:4-7).
  • A heart that accepts grace is changed and bears fruit: “faith without works is dead” (James 2:26). Catholics see good works not as a way of buying salvation but as the response to a love already received.
A skeptic might askSo a bad person can repent at the last minute and be saved? Is that fair?

Answer: Yes: Jesus told the criminal crucified beside him, “Today you will be with me in paradise” (Luke 23:43). Grace goes beyond fairness, because none of us deserves it; we only see it as unfair when we imagine we do. But it isn't a license to keep sinning: repentance means a real turning, a change of heart, and no one is guaranteed tomorrow.

Mary and the Saints

Do Catholics worship Mary and the saints?

No: worship belongs to God alone; we ask the saints to pray for us, just as we would ask a friend.

  • Scripture says the prayer of a righteous person is very powerful (James 5:16). Our brothers and sisters in heaven are closer to God than we are; death doesn't separate those who are one in Christ.
  • The special honour given to Mary is because she is Jesus' mother and first disciple; she always points people to her Son: “Do whatever he tells you” (John 2:5).
  • The saints are proof that the Christian life can really be lived: people from every culture, age and temperament, many of whom, like Augustine, were great sinners who were transformed.
A skeptic might askWhy not pray to God directly?

Answer: We do; the centre of Catholic prayer is the Mass, offered to the Father through Christ. Asking someone to pray for us doesn't replace praying to God directly; it's love added to it. If asking a friend to pray for you doesn't reduce your trust in God, neither does asking a friend in heaven.

Step 5 of 5

Morality and Destiny

Human beings were made for an endless happiness; the Church's moral teaching isn't a list of prohibitions but a map of the road there.

The Heart's Longing

Do I really need God?

No achievement, wealth or relationship fully satisfies our hearts, and that is a sign we were made for something more.

  • Hunger points to the existence of food, thirst to water; each of our natural desires has something that answers it.
  • C. S. Lewis wrote: “If I find in myself a desire which no experience in this world can satisfy, the most probable explanation is that I was made for another world.”
  • Saint Augustine, after years of searching in pleasure, success and philosophy, reached the same conclusion: “You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you.”
A skeptic might askThat's just a need for comfort; that's why people invent religion.

Answer: Needing something doesn't show it isn't real; being thirsty doesn't prove water is an illusion. And Christianity is hardly a cozy comfort: it asks us to love our enemies, to forgive and to give ourselves up, and it gathers around a cross. If people were inventing a religion just to feel good, they probably wouldn't have chosen one this demanding.

Moral Truths

Why are the Church's rules so strict?

The Church's moral teaching isn't a set of arbitrary bans but a consistent understanding of what a human being is; its aim is to free, not to restrict.

  • Every human life, from the child in the womb to the elderly and the sick, is made in God's image and inviolable; a person's worth doesn't depend on their usefulness.
  • The body isn't an accessory but the person themselves; that is why sexuality finds its full meaning within a lifelong promise, marriage.
  • We don't call a train “unfree” because it's bound to its rails; the rails are how it reaches its destination. Morality, too, guides a person to live according to their nature and toward real happiness.
A skeptic might askAs long as I'm not hurting anyone, what I do is nobody's business.

Answer: The Church doesn't force; it invites. But “not hurting anyone” isn't a sufficient measure of a good life. We can harm ourselves, and what we do affects others in ways we don't see. And the Church doesn't shut out those who struggle on this road: that's exactly what Confession is for, beginning again after every fall.

Heaven, Hell and Purgatory

How could a loving God send anyone to hell?

God forces no one into heaven or hell; eternity is the permanent form of the free answer we give in this life.

  • Heaven isn't a garden of rewards but endless union with God, the source of all goodness, beauty and love.
  • Hell is the result of freely and persistently choosing to stay away from God forever. As C. S. Lewis said, the doors of hell are locked on the inside.
  • Purgatory is a purification in which those who die in God's grace but aren't yet fully purified are made ready for heaven; not a punishment but love being completed. The Jews of the Maccabean period, too, prayed for their dead (2 Maccabees 12:46).
A skeptic might askIsn't eternal punishment out of proportion to sins committed in a limited life?

Answer: Hell isn't God's endless revenge for a mistake; it is a will that rejected God to the very end continuing that rejection forever. God keeps the door open until the last moment and wants no one to be lost (1 Timothy 2:4). The Church has never declared that any particular person is in hell; but it teaches that the possibility is real, because our freedom is real.

Putting it together

  1. 1There is real truth
  2. 2The universe points to a Creator
  3. 3Jesus rose from the dead
  4. 4His Church is still alive today
  5. 5Our hearts find rest in him

These five steps aren't mathematical proofs on their own, but clues that support one another: if there is real truth, we can seek it; if the universe points to a Creator, he may have made himself known; if Jesus rose, what he said is true; and the Church he founded is still alive today, two thousand years on, with its saints and its sinners. The final step of faith isn't taken by reason alone, but reason can bring us to its threshold. If you have questions, you're not alone: you can knock on the door of any Catholic church, or write to us.
