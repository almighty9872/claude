About This Site

This English section is a growing, partial mirror of that Turkish site; most of it is still Turkish-only for now, and pages are being translated over time.

The icon in the bottom-right corner of every page opens an accessibility menu, with ready-made profiles for motor impairment, blindness, color blindness and dyslexia, plus individual settings for contrast, text size, letter spacing and a screen-reader helper (details are on the Accessibility page).

Miracles

The truths at the core of the Catholic faith, such as Christ's death, resurrection and real presence in the Eucharist, are grounded in the Church's official teaching, that is, in Sacred Tradition and Sacred Scripture. The apparitions and extraordinary events described below fall into the category of "private revelation": they add nothing to the Church's teaching, but they are events the Church, after lengthy investigation, has found "worthy of belief" and permits to be honored with respect. What follows relates the history of these events and the notable scientific findings behind them.

1Marian Apparitions

Church history records many testimonies of the Virgin Mary appearing to people at various places and times. The Church has formally approved only a few of these, after lengthy investigation. The four below are the best known worldwide and the most widely visited.

Our Lady of FatimaFatima, Portugal · 1917

On May 13, 1917, three shepherd children in central Portugal, ten-year-old Lucia dos Santos and her cousins Francisco (8) and Jacinta Marto (6), reported seeing a woman surrounded by light. The woman identified herself as "the Lady of the Rosary" and asked the children to return to the same spot on the thirteenth of each month for the next five months. The apparitions continued through October, with the crowd growing each time as word spread quickly.

At the final apparition on October 13, 1917, an estimated thirty to a hundred thousand people had gathered at Cova da Iria, including skeptical journalists. The crowd witnessed an extraordinary event in which the sun appeared to spin, change color, and lurch toward the earth, later called the "Miracle of the Sun." Francisco and Jacinta died shortly after in the 1918-1919 flu pandemic; Lucia became a nun and died in 2005 at ninety-seven.

The Bishop of Leiria declared the apparitions "worthy of belief" in 1930, after a thirteen-year investigation. Fatima is today one of the most visited Marian pilgrimage sites in the world; millions visit the basilica at Cova da Iria every year. Francisco and Jacinta were canonized in 2017, based on Lucia's testimony.

Our Lady of ZeitounCairo, Egypt · 1968-1971

On the evening of April 2, 1968, a glowing white figure of a woman made of light was seen on the dome of a Coptic church in the Zeitoun district of Cairo. The first witnesses were two Muslim bus mechanics working in the garage across from the church; thinking someone was about to jump from the dome, they called for help. The crowd that gathered noticed the figure of light resembled the Virgin Mary, and word spread quickly.

The apparitions repeated at irregular intervals over the next three years, especially on nights near Church feast days. It's estimated that nearly a million people over this period, including Christians, Muslims and foreign journalists, saw the figure of a woman made of light above the dome along with dove-shaped clusters of light. Unlike Lourdes or Fatima, the Zeitoun apparitions carried no spoken message and no speaking visionary; it's described as a silent presence of light.

According to Coptic tradition, Zeitoun is one of the places the Holy Family stopped during the flight into Egypt. Coptic Orthodox Pope Kyrillos VI set up a commission to investigate the event and formally approved the apparitions; the Catholic Patriarch of Cairo at the time, Stephanos I, also stated he had no doubt about the reliability of the witnesses. The Zeitoun apparitions never went through the Roman Catholic Church's own formal approval process, since the event took place at a Coptic church; even so, it's honored with respect by both Coptic and Catholic communities.

Our Lady of GuadalupeMexico City, Mexico · 1531

In December 1531, about ten years after the Spanish conquest, a recently baptized indigenous Aztec man named Juan Diego reported seeing a woman on Tepeyac hill near Mexico City. The woman identified herself in the Nahuatl language and asked Juan Diego to go to Bishop Juan de Zumárraga and ask him to build a church for her on that hill.

When the bishop wasn't convinced, the woman told Juan Diego to ask for a sign. In the middle of winter, he found the hill covered with Spanish roses that don't normally grow in that climate; he gathered them and carried them to the bishop wrapped in his cloak (tilma). When he opened his cloak, the roses fell to the ground, and on the inside of the cloak appeared the image of the Virgin Mary that's still seen today.

On seeing this, the bishop had the requested church built. The image of Guadalupe is credited with playing a major role in the conversion of millions of indigenous people to Christianity in the decades that followed; because the image is filled with symbols readable in Aztec pictorial language, it's interpreted as speaking directly to indigenous people who couldn't read. Today declared the patroness of Mexico and of the whole American continent, Our Lady of Guadalupe is honored at the world's most visited Marian pilgrimage site, the basilica in Mexico City.

Our Lady of LourdesLourdes, France · 1858

On February 11, 1858, a fourteen-year-old poor and asthmatic girl named Bernadette Soubirous, living in the town of Lourdes in southern France, was gathering firewood near the Massabielle grotto when she heard a sound like wind and saw a young woman at the mouth of the grotto, dressed in white with a blue sash, with a golden rose on each foot. Over eighteen apparitions that continued until July 16, the woman gave Bernadette no special message for daily life; she called her to prayer, penance, and to drink from a spring in the grotto.

At the apparition on March 25, when Bernadette asked the woman's identity, she was told, "I am the Immaculate Conception." This wasn't a phrase a peasant girl with no theological education could have known at the time; what's more, only four years had passed since Pope Pius IX had declared Mary's Immaculate Conception a dogma of the faith. This detail was a major factor in the Church taking the apparitions seriously.

A spring appeared at the spot Bernadette pointed to; millions of pilgrims still come to Lourdes today to drink from or bathe in this water. The Lourdes Medical Bureau examines thousands of recorded healing claims with independent doctors; only around sixty of these have been formally recognized by the Church as medically inexplicable "miracles." The Church approved the apparitions in 1862, after investigation. Bernadette became a nun in 1866, settled in Nevers, and died in 1879 at thirty-five; she was canonized in 1933.

2Sacred Relics & Physical Artifacts

Catholic tradition is also known for certain sacred relics and objects that have undergone scientific examination for centuries. Below, we look at the two objects scientists have studied the most.

The Shroud of TurinTurin, Italy

The Shroud of Turin is a linen cloth roughly 4.4 meters long, bearing the front and back image of a naked man's body that was scourged and crucified. Church tradition holds this to be the shroud Christ was wrapped in when laid in the tomb. The Shroud's first documented appearance in history is in the 1350s, in the French town of Lirey; it has been kept in Turin, at the Cathedral of St. John the Baptist, since 1578.

When the Shroud was first photographed in 1898, it was found that the image on the negative showed a far clearer human face and body than the faint marks on the cloth itself; the mark on the cloth behaved exactly like a photographic negative. This is a property medieval craftsmen couldn't have known, since the negative-positive relationship was only understood with the advent of photography in the 19th century. In 1978, the STURP (Shroud of Turin Research Project) team of about thirty scientists examined the Shroud directly for five days and conclusively established that the image was not formed by paint, pigment, dye or brushstroke.

Microscopic examination shows the straw-yellow color that makes up the image is found only in the outermost layer of the linen fibers, the primary cell wall. The depth of this coloration is between 200 and 500 nanometers, thinner even than the wavelength of visible light; the core of the fibers remains entirely white, with no trace of any liquid or dye having penetrated the fabric.

Spectroscopic and chemical analyses by STURP confirmed the image contains no paint, ink, heavy metal, or organic binder. According to the findings, the coloration is the result of an oxidation, dehydration and conjugation process occurring in the linen's own cellulose; it's resistant to water, standard solvents, and heat up to the linen's scorch point.

In 1976, when NASA engineers examined the Shroud image with a device called a VP-8 Image Analyzer, designed to map elevation differences in space photographs, they discovered that variations in the grey tones were directly proportional to the distance between the cloth and a three-dimensional body. An ordinary painting or photograph processed this way produces a distorted, meaningless relief; the Shroud's image instead produces an anatomically consistent, genuine three-dimensional relief. This property has never been reproduced in any other painting or cloth to date. Under the microscope, the image also shows no directional brushstroke or line boundary; the coloration also carries no capillary flow marks, meaning it wasn't formed by a liquid, vapor, ointment or chemical, since such substances always leave a flow mark as they spread through the weave of a cloth.

Forensic examination of the blood stains has confirmed they were formed by real blood, containing hemoglobin, bilirubin and human serum, transferring to the cloth by direct contact. Around the stains are serum rings invisible to the naked eye but visible under ultraviolet light. Most notably, there is no coloration belonging to the body image beneath the blood stains, showing that the blood was on the cloth before the body image formed.

In 1988, three laboratories at Oxford, Zurich and Arizona ran a radiocarbon (C-14) test on a small sample taken from just one corner of the cloth, and found a date of AD 1260-1390. However, the corner the sample was taken from isn't representative of the Shroud's main fabric: this area is an edge secretly repaired by nuns after the 1532 fire at Chambéry, by weaving new thread into the original cloth. In 2005, STURP's senior chemist, Raymond Rogers, a former Los Alamos National Laboratory researcher, found in a study published in Thermochimica Acta that the test sample contained cotton fibers not found in the rest of the Shroud, and that traces of dye in the sample matched a known dyeing technique used to color-match medieval repairs with the rest of the cloth. Vanillin loss in the Shroud's main fabric was also far more advanced than in the tested corner, indicating the main fabric is much older than the tested corner. Based on these findings, the 1988 test may have dated not the Shroud itself, but a silent repair made centuries later.

One of the most striking answers to the question of how the image formed comes from research into electromagnetic radiation. A team led by Paolo Di Lazzaro at ENEA, Italy's national energy agency, showed that very short, high-power vacuum ultraviolet (VUV) laser pulses (at a wavelength of 193 nanometers) could reproduce, in laboratory conditions, the straw-yellow color and shallow surface depth found in the Shroud's fibers. The researchers note that producing a complete image at human scale this way would require an energy equivalent to thousands of today's excimer lasers firing simultaneously within a tiny fraction of a second, an energy level no known source in antiquity or the Middle Ages could have produced. Other researchers propose that high-voltage electrical fields (corona discharge) could dehydrate only the outermost layer of linen fibers without burning the core; some models, in order to explain both the three-dimensional depth information and the sharp clarity of the image together, also assume a brief burst of radiation or charged particle flux emitted vertically from the body itself.

Taken together, these findings suggest the Shroud of Turin isn't an ordinary medieval painting or a forgery, but bears an extraordinary mark formed by direct contact with a real human body, one that even today's science can't fully explain. The Catholic Church has never formally declared the Shroud "authentic," because this isn't directly a matter of faith but an ongoing subject of scientific research; but popes have visited it regularly and honored it with deep reverence, and for centuries it has been kept in Turin as a sacred relic on which believers meditate on Christ's Passion and Resurrection.

The Guadalupe TilmaMexico City, Mexico

Juan Diego's cloak (tilma) is a coarse fabric woven from the fiber of the maguey plant, a relative of the cactus; a fabric like this would normally be expected to decay and fall apart within a few decades. Despite this, the tilma has been kept in a state where the image is still clearly visible today, even though it was displayed for centuries without a protective glass case; this durability is one of the most frequently cited extraordinary aspects of the image.

In 1751, the painter Miguel Cabrera examined the image and wrote that he couldn't understand how such fine detail had been painted onto so coarse a fabric. In 1979, an infrared examination by biophysicist Philip Callahan found no trace of a brush sketch or layered paint in the main body of the image, while the moon, angel, stars and rays were found to have been added later, between the late 16th and 17th centuries. Engineer José Aste Tonsmann, examining Mary's pupils under high magnification, reported seeing small figures of the people standing before her at that moment, just as a living eye would reflect.

The oldest known written account of the event is the Nican Mopohua text, published in Nahuatl in 1649; supporters also point to a document called the Codex Escalada, which pushes the event to a much earlier date, the 1530s. As the most sacred object of Catholic Mexico, the tilma continues to be visited by more than twenty million pilgrims every year.

3Eucharistic Miracles

In Catholic belief, at the words of consecration the bread and wine of the Eucharist become the real body and blood of Christ; this is called transubstantiation. This change can't be perceived by the senses; the bread and wine appear completely unchanged from the outside. In the three events below, tradition holds that this change became visible.

The Miracle of LancianoLanciano, Italy · 8th century

According to tradition, in the 8th century a Basilian monk-priest in Lanciano, Italy, was doubting whether Christ was truly present in the Eucharist. The moment he spoke the words of consecration during Mass, the bread in his hands turned into living flesh, and the wine into blood; the horrified priest showed the congregation what had happened.

The flesh and blood are still displayed today at the Church of St. Francis in Lanciano, in a silver vessel. In 1970-71, pathologist Odoardo Linoli examined the tissue under a microscope and reported that the flesh was human cardiac muscle tissue, the blood was human blood of type AB, and that despite no preservative having been used, the tissue had remained undecayed for twelve centuries.

The Miracle of Bolsena-OrvietoBolsena / Orvieto, Italy · 1263

In 1263, a Bohemian priest named Peter, on pilgrimage to Rome, is recounted to have been doubting the real presence in the Eucharist while celebrating Mass in the town of Bolsena. The moment he broke the bread, he saw blood seeping onto the corporal, the linen altar cloth used at that Mass. Shaken, the priest reported the event to Pope Urban IV, who was nearby in Orvieto.

The Pope had the event investigated and had the bloodied cloth brought to Orvieto. The following year, 1264, moved by this event, Pope Urban IV instituted the universal feast of Corpus Christi (the Body of Christ) honoring the Eucharist for the whole Church, and commissioned St. Thomas Aquinas to write the prayers for the feast. This event is considered one of the most solidly documented Eucharistic miracles in Church history, since it's tied directly to a papal investigation and to the founding of a universal feast still celebrated today.

The bloodied cloth is kept today in the special Chapel of the Corporal at Orvieto Cathedral, in a gold and enameled silver reliquary. Every year on the feast of Corpus Christi, the relic is carried in a great procession through the streets of the city.

The Buenos Aires MiracleBuenos Aires, Argentina · 1996

In August 1996, a consecrated host was found left in a collection box at a church in Buenos Aires. Following church custom, Father Alejandro Pezet placed the host in a container of water to dissolve it respectfully and kept it in the tabernacle. A few days later, on August 26, it was noticed that red, blood-like stains had appeared on the host, and that the stains were growing larger day by day.

The Archbishop of Buenos Aires at the time, Jorge Mario Bergoglio, the future Pope Francis, had the matter scientifically examined. The matter remained undecayed for three years with no special preservation measures taken; a sample taken in 1999 was examined in New York by cardiologist Frederic Zugiba, without being told its source. The report found the tissue to be human cardiac muscle from the left ventricle of an inflamed heart, with blood type AB, the same type reported for the Shroud of Turin and for Lanciano.

Even after becoming pope, Bergoglio has often spoken of this event as an example of his deep reverence for the Eucharist. The relic is still kept with reverence at the same church today.

4Incorruptible Saints

The bodies of some saints have been found far less decayed than expected years, even centuries, after their death; the Church carefully documents this extraordinary preservation and records it with reverence. In the three examples below, we also explain that after this initial miraculous preservation, the Church had a thin wax or silicone mask made for the bodies so they could be displayed permanently; this is part of the story of the relics that are visited with reverence today.

St. Bernadette of LourdesNevers, France

Bernadette Soubirous, the visionary of the Lourdes apparitions, joined the Sisters of Charity convent in Nevers in 1866 and died in 1879 at thirty-five, from long-suffered bone tuberculosis and asthma. As part of the canonization process, her body was exhumed three times: in 1909, 1919, and 1925.

At the first exhumation in 1909, witnesses reported that the skin looked surprisingly natural, with the mouth slightly open and the teeth still in place, an unexpected finding thirty years after burial. At the second examination in 1919, no sign of decay was found, though the face and hands had darkened from the washing done at the first examination. At the third examination in 1925, the Church decided to display the body permanently; for this, Pierre Imans, a Paris designer of fashion mannequins, was commissioned to make a thin wax mask for the face and hands to cover the sunken eyes and nose and the darkened skin.

The body displayed today in a glass reliquary at the Saint Gildard Chapel in Nevers is seen with this wax mask; beneath the mask, the body itself, which remained undecayed for decades, still lies. Bernadette was canonized in 1933.

St. Padre PioSan Giovanni Rotondo, Italy

Francesco Forgione was known as a Capuchin friar in Italy under the name Padre Pio; from 1918 until his death in 1968, tradition holds that he bore stigmata, wounds resembling Christ's crucifixion wounds, on his hands, feet and side. These wounds were examined repeatedly throughout his life by doctors appointed by the Church; many witnesses reported that the wounds continued to bleed for fifty years, never became infected, and gave off a scent of fresh roses ("osmogenesis").

Padre Pio was canonized in 2002. On the fortieth anniversary of his death, in 2008, his body was exhumed with a scientific team present. The hands, feet and parts of the face were found well preserved, but the top of the skull had become skeletal, so a full silicone mask was made for the face. The body was also chemically preserved with a formalin-alcohol mixture.

Displayed today at the new church in San Giovanni Rotondo, in a special glass reliquary with controlled humidity and temperature, the body is seen with this silicone mask; millions of pilgrims come here every year in memory of this extraordinary sign Padre Pio bore throughout his life.

St. Jean-Marie Vianney (The Curé of Ars)Ars, France

Jean-Marie Vianney was a parish priest who served for more than forty years in the small French village of Ars in the 19th century; he's known for hearing confessions up to sixteen hours a day, with people flocking to his confessional from all over France. He's since been declared the patron saint of all parish priests.

After his death in 1859, his body was exhumed forty-five years later, in 1904, for the canonization process, and was found dried and darkened but not decomposed. That same year his heart was removed as a separate relic and placed in its own reliquary; this incorrupt heart is still displayed today in a separate chapel and is occasionally taken abroad for veneration.

The rest of his body is displayed in a glass reliquary at the basilica in Ars; his face is covered with a wax mask made to conceal the darkening. Vianney was canonized in 1925.
