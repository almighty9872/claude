About This Site

katolikdunyasi.com was built to make the core texts and teaching of the Catholic faith accessible in Turkish: the Compendium of the Catechism, the process of becoming Catholic, a confession guide, the Mass, the parables of Jesus, prayers, a calendar of the saints and the twenty best-known among them, Catholic miracles, the roots of our faith in Anatolia, a section grounding the faith in reason, the Bible, frequently asked questions, and a directory of active Catholic churches in Turkey. This English section is a growing, partial mirror of that Turkish site; most of it is still Turkish-only for now, and pages are being translated over time.

The icon in the bottom-right corner of every page opens an accessibility menu, with ready-made profiles for motor impairment, blindness, color blindness and dyslexia, plus individual settings for contrast, text size, letter spacing and a screen-reader helper (details are on the Accessibility page).

Becoming Catholic

Becoming Catholic is one of the most meaningful steps in a person's life. Some receive this great gift as infants and grow into an awareness of it over the years; others join the Catholic faith in childhood or as adults. A person enters full communion with the Church by receiving the three sacraments of Christian initiation, baptism, confirmation and the Holy Eucharist, though the shape of that process varies depending on their situation.

1Two Paths

If you've never been baptized before

You become Catholic the moment you're baptized. Confirmation and the Eucharist deepen that beginning, but entry into the Church happens through baptism. The process that prepares you for this step is described below.

If you were validly baptized in another Christian community

You're already Christian; you join the Catholic Church by professing your faith and being formally received. This is usually followed, in the same liturgy, by confirmation and first Holy Communion. Your preparation differs from that of someone who's never been baptized; the details are below.

2The Process, Step by Step

The process preparing unbaptized people and older children (over age seven) for reception into the Church is now called OCIA (the Order of Christian Initiation of Adults; formerly RCIA, the Rite of Christian Initiation of Adults). Though it was given its current shape by the Church in 1972, the tradition of preparing candidates for the faith goes back to the Church's founding at Pentecost; even in the 2nd century, St. Hippolytus wrote of candidates passing through three stages. The process usually begins in September and runs about six months, ending at the Easter Vigil. Anyone wanting to begin should contact their nearest parish.

  1. 1

    Inquiry

    The unbaptized person begins learning about the Catholic faith and decides whether to embrace it. This is a period open to questions, with no commitment required.

  2. 2

    Rite of Reception into the Order of Catechumens

    The first formal step of the process. The candidate openly states their desire and intention to become Christian, and from this point on is called a catechumen (a person preparing for baptism).

  3. 3

    The Catechumenate

    Usually lasting less than a year, this period gives the catechumen thorough formation in Catholic doctrine and morality, along with the chance to deepen their faith and confirm their desire to become Catholic.

  4. 4

    The Rite of Election

    Usually celebrated on the first Sunday of Lent, the season of preparation for Easter. The catechumen's name is entered in the book of those who will receive the sacraments of initiation that year, and the Church judges the person ready.

  5. 5

    The Scrutinies

    Celebrated on the 3rd, 4th and 5th Sundays of Lent, usually during Mass. These rites are oriented toward self-examination and repentance, aiming to heal what's weak or sinful in the candidate's soul and strengthen what's good. During this period the candidate is formally presented with the Creed and the Lord's Prayer, which they will recite themselves on the night they receive the sacraments of initiation.

  6. 6

    The Easter Vigil

    In a special Mass celebrated on the eve of Easter night, the candidate is first baptized, then confirmed, and finally receives Holy Communion for the first time. This is how they become Catholic and enter full communion with the Church. Ideally the bishop presides in person, but distance or the number of candidates usually leaves this to the parish priest.

  7. 7

    Mystagogy

    This continues through the fifty days of the Easter season, between Easter and Pentecost. New Catholics are strengthened in faith through further teaching and put down deeper roots in the parish community. For the first year, they're called neophytes (newly Christian).

3If you're already a baptized Christian

Someone previously and validly baptized is already Christian, so they aren't counted as a catechumen and don't take part in rites specific to catechumens, such as the scrutinies or the formal presentation of the Creed; those are suited only to people who've never received any Christian formation.

If they were baptized but never lived as a Christian or received any formation, formation similar to that given to catechumens is appropriate, though they still aren't called a catechumen. If they've already lived in faith and only need instruction in Catholic tradition specifically, a full program parallel to the catechumenate isn't needed; preparation is tailored to the individual.

These people's reception into the Church is usually celebrated not at the Easter Vigil but at an ordinary Sunday Mass of the parish; this avoids confusion with baptismal candidates and doesn't cast any shadow on the baptism they received in another community. Reception is usually completed, in the same liturgy, with confirmation and first Holy Communion.

Because baptism erases all previous sin, confession doesn't come into play for someone not yet baptized; but someone already baptized must confess, separately and before their reception into the Church, any mortal sins committed after their baptism. If many years passed between baptism and reception, the person confesses the mortal sins they remember by kind and, where possible, frequency; the absolution still covers what can't be remembered, as long as there's a sincere intention of sorrow for all mortal sins.

At the reception rite the candidate makes this profession of faith: "I believe and profess all that the holy Catholic Church believes, teaches, and proclaims to be revealed by God." The bishop or priest then receives them into the Church with these words: "[Name], the Lord receives you into the Catholic Church. His loving kindness has led you here, so that in the unity of the Holy Spirit you may have full communion with us in the faith that you have professed in the presence of your family."

4When there's doubt about a baptism's validity

Regardless of denomination, every baptism is presumed valid unless proven otherwise. After serious investigation, if there's a serious doubt that a person was baptized with water and in the name of the Trinity ("in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit"), or that the minister or the one baptized intended it as a real baptism, a conditional baptism is administered: "If you are not already baptized, I baptize you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit."

Special preparation is also provided for adults baptized Catholic but who never received any religious formation, or who never received confirmation and the Eucharist. These people aren't counted as catechumens, but some elements of catechumenate formation can be adapted for their preparation for confirmation and the Eucharist.

5Is the waiting period wasted time?

Waiting for the warm embrace of full membership in the Church can sometimes feel like a trial of patience. But this period of waiting and reflection is necessary, because becoming Catholic is a life-changing event.

For those already Christian, baptism itself establishes a definite sacramental bond with the Church (see Vatican II, Unitatis Redintegratio 3; CCC 1271). Even before baptism, catechumens who sincerely desire to join the Church are already bound to it by that very desire: "Catechumens who, moved by the Holy Spirit, desire with an explicit intention to be incorporated into the Church are by that very intention joined with her. With love and solicitude Mother Church already embraces them as her own" (Vatican II, Lumen Gentium 14; CCC 1249).

6Practical Questions

How do I start the OCIA process?

Get in touch with the nearest Catholic parish. The process usually begins in September, with confirmation taking place at the Easter Vigil; that means roughly six months of preparation. That said, if you reach out to the parish priest, or the parish office if there is one, in early August, you won't be late. You'll likely need a face-to-face meeting with the pastor; this is simply so they can get to know you and learn more about your reasons for joining the Church. Every parish is different, though, so your experience may vary.

I was baptized Catholic but never received any religious formation; what should I do?

You aren't counted as a catechumen, since you're already baptized. But as you prepare for confirmation and the Eucharist, some parts of catechumenate formation are adapted for you too. Talk to your nearest parish.

If I'm already Christian, why aren't I received at the Easter Vigil?

This is to avoid confusing you with candidates who aren't yet baptized, and to avoid casting any shadow on the baptism you received in another community. Your reception is usually celebrated at an ordinary Sunday Mass of the parish.

Do I need to go to confession?

If you're already baptized, yes; before your reception into the Church, and at a separate time, you're expected to confess, so that you're received in a state of grace and can receive confirmation. This doesn't apply if you've never been baptized, because baptism itself erases all previous sin. See our step-by-step confession guide to learn more about how it works.

I'm not sure whether my baptism was valid; what happens?

Every baptism is presumed valid until a serious doubt arises. If there's doubt, your priest examines the situation, and a conditional baptism is administered if needed: "If you are not already baptized, I baptize you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit."

Am I kept apart from the Church during the waiting period?

No. If you're already baptized, your baptism itself has established a real bond with the Church (CCC 1271). Even if you're not yet baptized, your intention to join the Church already binds you to it; Mother Church already regards you with love as her own child (CCC 1249).

The general OCIA process on this page is a universal Church regulation (1972, Congregation for Divine Worship); some details above (such as reception outside the Easter Vigil, or the timing of confession) are drawn from the U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops' National Statutes for the Catechumenate (1986). For practice in your own region, ask your nearest parish.
