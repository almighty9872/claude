## What makes an edition Catholic?

The Catholic Bible has 73 books. Protestant editions are missing seven books from the Old Testament: Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and 1 and 2 Maccabees, along with parts of Esther and Daniel. These are called the *deuterocanonical* books, and the Church has held them to be inspired Scripture from the beginning.

There are two things to check before buying:

- The words **"Catholic Edition"** on the cover. A printing of the same translation without this label may not include those seven books.
- **Nihil obstat** and **Imprimatur** on the inside cover. These show the translation carries the Church's approval.

The authority to approve isn't arbitrary: the 1983 Code of Canon Law gives the authority to approve translations of Scripture to the Holy See and to conferences of bishops (canon 825, §1).

## No approved Catholic edition exists in Turkish yet

There is currently no printed Turkish Catholic Bible edition carrying church approval (Nihil obstat/Imprimatur). The closest option is the Bible Society in Turkey's 2003 *Ecumenical* translation, **Kutsal Kitap ve Deuterokanonik Kitaplar** ("The Bible and the Deuterocanonical Books"). This edition includes the seven deuterocanonical books used by the Catholic and Orthodox Churches, in contemporary, easy-to-read Turkish.

Two places to read it for free:

- [Kutsal Kitap ve Deuterokanonik Kitaplar (KKDEU), Bible.com](https://www.bible.com/tr/versions/2308-kkdeu-kutsal-kitap-ve-deuterokanonik-kitaplar)
- [kitabimukaddes.com/tr/kutsal-kitap](https://kitabimukaddes.com/tr/kutsal-kitap)

## Why are there so many translations?

Because translators follow two different approaches.

A **formal (word-for-word) translation** stays as close as possible to the original text, often preserving even word order. A **dynamic (thought-for-thought) translation** aims to convey meaning rather than grammar, giving the translator more freedom to write natural, contemporary English.

The difference shows up in practice: the Hebrew idiom in Leviticus 18 is rendered by the formal Douay-Rheims translation as "uncover the nakedness of," preserving the original idiom as it stands; more dynamic translations render the same verse with a direct, everyday phrase like "have sexual relations with." Both say the same thing, but one keeps the idiom and the other carries the meaning into everyday language.

## Which is better for serious study?

A formal translation. A verse's meaning often depends on subtle cues in the text, and only formal translations preserve those cues. The cost is readability: because Hebrew and Greek style bleeds through into the text, it's harder to read.

## Which is better for daily reading?

A dynamic, or middle-of-the-road, translation. You'll read faster and grasp the text's main meaning more easily. The cost is precision: detail is lost, and there's a greater risk of the translator's own views creeping into the text.

## Can translation change doctrine?

Yes, it can. A well-known example is the Greek word *ergon* ("work, deed"). In some Protestant translations this word is rendered "works" in Romans 4:2, while words from the same root are given a different English rendering in Romans 2:6-7. Translated consistently, the text would plainly say that God will judge everyone *according to their works*, and give eternal life to *those who persevere in doing good*, which is to say, the Catholic understanding of salvation.

Even without a doctrinal motive, the sacred writer's intent can become blurred in dynamic translations.

## Where does each translation fall?

The ranking below covers only Catholic editions (translations carrying both the deuterocanonical books and church approval):

- **Closest to formal:** Douay-Rheims (Challoner revision)
- **Formal, more readable:** Revised Standard Version, Catholic Edition (RSV-CE and RSV-2CE), Confraternity Bible
- **Middle of the road:** New American Bible, Revised Edition (NABRE), New Jerusalem Bible (NJB)
- **Closer to dynamic:** New Revised Standard Version, Catholic Edition (NRSV-CE), Good News Translation (Catholic edition)

One note: the **NRSV-CE** is hard to place on this scale; its base text is formal, but it uses "gender-inclusive language," meaning where the original text says "brothers," it writes "brothers and sisters" with a footnote. **Free paraphrases** (such as translations of the Living Bible) aren't translations at all; they're a retelling of another translation, and carry no church approval.

## Are older translations more reliable?

No, only older. They can seem dignified and impressive, but because they were written in English from four hundred years ago, they're hard to read.

Some claim "only the Douay-Rheims is accurate, the others can't be trusted"; this claim is false. No manuscript set is flawless, and none of the editions sold today is the original 1600s text: the Douay sold today is technically the **Douay-Challoner**, because it was revised in the eighteenth century by Bishop Richard Challoner, who also consulted the Greek and Hebrew manuscripts.

## So which should I get?

Stay away from translations that use unusual renderings; the Good News Bible is known for this ("the abomination of desolation" becomes "the awful horror," and the Ark of the Covenant becomes "the covenant box").

For general use, the **Revised Standard Version, Catholic Edition (RSV-CE)** is recommended. Ignatius Press publishes it as **The Ignatius Bible**.

You don't have to settle on just one translation, either. Keeping a few editions side by side is one of the best ways to understand what a verse is really saying.

Which one is best? The truest answer is: **the one you'll actually read.**

## Approved translations

The full list of English translations approved by the United States Conference of Catholic Bishops (USCCB) since 1983:

- Books of the New Testament, Alba House
- Contemporary English Version, New Testament, First Edition, American Bible Society
- Contemporary English Version, Book of Psalms, American Bible Society
- Contemporary English Version, Book of Proverbs, American Bible Society
- The Grail Psalter (Inclusive Language Version), G.I.A. Publications
- **New American Bible, Revised Edition (NABRE)**
- **New Revised Standard Version, Catholic Edition**, National Council of Churches
- **New Revised Standard Version, Updated Edition, Catholic Edition**, Friendship Press
- The Psalms, Alba House
- The Psalms (New International Version), St. Joseph Catholic Edition, Catholic Book Publishing Company
- The Psalms, St. Joseph New Catholic Version, Catholic Book Publishing Company
- Revised Psalms of the New American Bible (1991)
- So You May Believe, A Translation of the Four Gospels, Alba House
- Good News Translation (Today's English Version, Second Edition), American Bible Society
- Translation for Early Youth, A Translation of the New Testament for Children, Contemporary English Version, American Bible Society

This list binds only the US conference, and covers only contemporary translations. The **Douay-Rheims (Challoner revision)** doesn't appear on it for that reason, but it carries its own historical church approval and remains a legitimate, usable Catholic translation today.

Any translation with due church approval can be used for private prayer and study; if you live in another country, check your own bishops' conference's list.
